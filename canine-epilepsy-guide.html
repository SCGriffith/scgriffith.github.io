Epilepsy is the most common chronic neurological condition in dogs. This guide walks through how seizures are classified, what treats them, what's still theory, and how to keep the record your vet actually needs.

Before anything else: this page is educational, not diagnostic or prescriptive. Never start, stop, or change an anticonvulsant, diet, or supplement without your veterinarian or a veterinary neurologist — abrupt changes to seizure medication can themselves trigger dangerous breakthrough seizures.
01 · Overview

What "epilepsy" actually covers

Epilepsy isn't one disease — it's a tendency toward recurrent, unprovoked seizures, and vets sort the cause into three buckets before deciding on treatment.

Most common

Idiopathic epilepsy

No structural cause is found on bloodwork or imaging; a genetic or inherited basis is suspected and confirmed in several breeds (Border Collie, Belgian Shepherd, Australian Shepherd, Labrador, Beagle, among others). Onset typically falls between 6 months and 6 years of age.

Structural

Structural epilepsy

Seizures arise from identifiable brain disease — a tumor, prior trauma, stroke, malformation, or inflammatory/infectious encephalitis. More likely when seizures start outside the typical idiopathic age window, or when neurological exam findings are abnormal between seizures.

Reactive

Reactive seizures

The brain itself is normal but is reacting to a metabolic derangement — low blood sugar, liver shunt/failure, kidney disease, electrolyte imbalance, or a toxin. Treating the underlying problem, not an anticonvulsant, is the fix here.

Diagnosis of exclusion

How vets sort it out

Bloodwork and often bile acids rule out reactive causes; MRI and spinal-fluid analysis look for structural disease. Idiopathic epilepsy is diagnosed when a dog fits the typical signalment and everything else comes back clean.

02 · The first-seizure question

One seizure: watch, or start treatment?

A single, brief seizure doesn't automatically mean a lifetime of daily medication. The decision to start — or to taper off later — follows its own logic, separate from diagnosing the seizure itself.

ASD — antiseizure drug (also called an anticonvulsant or antiepileptic drug/AED): any daily medication used to prevent seizures from occurring, as opposed to a rescue medication used to stop one already in progress.

Common default

"Wait and watch" after one event

Consensus guidance for dogs, extrapolated from strong human evidence, finds no proven long-term benefit to starting daily anticonvulsants after a single unprovoked seizure — dogs who wait tend to reach the same long-term seizure control as dogs treated immediately. Most vets hold off starting a daily ASD and instead ask you to keep a careful log (see the tracking section) to see whether it recurs.

Tips the scale toward treating now

When vets start sooner

Immediate treatment is favored if the first event was already a cluster (more than one seizure in 24 hours) or status epilepticus, if imaging shows structural brain disease, if seizures are closely spaced from the start, or if the dog's history and exam suggest a more aggressive course. These raise the odds that waiting costs more than it saves.

A different question entirely

Tapering doesn't "confirm" a diagnosis

Weaning a dog off an ASD is sometimes considered later in the course of disease — typically only after a long seizure-free stretch (often a year or more), to check whether medication is still needed or whether an underlying reactive cause has resolved. It isn't a diagnostic shortcut for a single early event: stopping a drug to "see if it comes back" right after one seizure isn't how epilepsy is confirmed, and it introduces a real risk that a wait-and-watch approach without medication doesn't have.

How tapering is actually done

Slow, planned, and supervised

When a taper is appropriate, it's done gradually over weeks to months, with your vet adjusting the schedule and watching for any return of seizure activity along the way — never stopped abruptly. This applies whether the goal is confirming ongoing need or simply reducing drug burden after a long stable stretch.

Why this matters  Suddenly stopping or rapidly lowering phenobarbital or potassium bromide can trigger withdrawal seizures or status epilepticus — sometimes worse than the disease being managed. Any taper, for any reason, should be planned and monitored by your vet, never done on your own timeline.

Before starting any taper, ask your vet about a rescue medication plan (such as at-home rectal or intranasal benzodiazepines) in case a breakthrough seizure happens while the dose is being lowered — having that plan in hand before you start, not after, means you're not scrambling mid-taper if one occurs.

03 · Seizure types

Generalized vs. focal — reading the trace

Vets classify by how much of the brain is involved at onset. That distinction changes what a seizure looks like, what it might mean, and sometimes which drugs work best.

Generalized (grand mal / tonic-clonic)

Both cerebral hemispheres are involved from the start. This is the seizure most owners picture and the one that's easiest to recognize.

  • Loss of consciousness, the dog collapses onto its side
  • Stiffening (tonic phase) followed by paddling/jerking limbs (clonic phase)
  • Drooling, chomping, sometimes loss of bladder or bowel control
  • Usually lasts under 2 minutes; anything past 5 minutes is an emergency (status epilepticus)
aura ictus (the event) post-ictal (mins–24h)

Focal (partial) seizures

Only one part of the brain fires. Signs are localized and easy to miss, mistake for behavior, or write off as "weird" — which is exactly why they're under-reported.

  • Facial twitching, one limb jerking, or repetitive blinking
  • Odd, sudden behavior: fly-snapping, air-licking, unexplained fear or aggression
  • Dog often stays conscious and aware throughout
  • Can stay focal, or "spread" into a secondarily generalized seizure
simple: awareness intact complex: awareness impaired

A video clip of an actual event is worth more to your vet than any description — see the tracking section below for what to capture.

Body temperature during a seizure

The muscle activity of a generalized seizure generates heat the same way hard exercise does. A single brief seizure rarely pushes temperature into dangerous territory, but prolonged seizures, cluster seizures, or status epilepticus can drive it up fast — and the resulting hyperthermia becomes its own emergency on top of the seizure itself.

Normal is a dog's ordinary resting range. Elevated is where prolonged muscle activity from a seizure commonly lands first — worth watching, not yet an emergency on its own. Severe is heatstroke territory: AAHA defines heatstroke as body temperature rising to dangerous levels, usually above 104°F, which can progress to organ damage if not addressed. Critical (above roughly 106°F/41°C) is where the risk of damage to the gut, liver, kidneys, heart, and clotting system rises sharply, and where death becomes a real possibility — this is a "go to the ER now" temperature, not a "monitor at home" one.

If cooling is started at home on the way to the vet, AAHA's guidance is to stop once the temperature comes down to about 103°F — cooling further than that risks an overcorrection into rebound hypothermia, so let the vet team take it from there.

04 · Conventional care

The standard anticonvulsants

Most dogs with idiopathic epilepsy are managed — not cured — with daily antiseizure drugs (ASDs), chosen by efficacy, side-effect profile, and how easily blood levels can be monitored.

DrugRoleWhat owners noticeMonitoring
Phenobarbital First-line; the most-prescribed ASD and among the best-studied Increased thirst, appetite, and urination; sedation early on that usually eases Blood levels + liver panel, typically checked periodically since the drug is processed by the liver
Potassium bromide First-line, often paired with phenobarbital in harder-to-control cases Sedation, wobbliness, increased thirst; takes months to reach steady levels Blood bromide levels; caution with kidney disease and salt intake, which shift bromide levels
Levetiracetam (Keppra) First-line or add-on; popular for a clean side-effect profile Usually well tolerated; occasional mild sedation or GI upset Given more often per day (immediate-release); levels can be checked but dosing is often clinical
Zonisamide Add-on or alternative first-line, especially if phenobarbital isn't tolerated Vomiting, wobbliness (ataxia); rare liver or kidney effects Liver values; caution in breeds prone to sulfonamide sensitivity
Imepitoin First-line option in some countries for milder, less-frequent seizures Generally mild sedation; less liver burden than phenobarbital No blood-level monitoring required, which is part of its appeal
Pregabalin / gabapentin Add-on for dogs not fully controlled on a primary ASD Sedation, wobbliness Kidney function, since both are cleared renally

Emergency care for cluster seizures or status epilepticus (rectal or intranasal diazepam/midazolam, or a hospital-administered constant-rate infusion) is planned in advance with your vet for dogs known to seize repeatedly in a short window.

05 · Alternative & adjunct approaches

Complementary options, honestly labeled

None of these replace an ASD in a dog that needs one. They're used alongside conventional treatment, and the evidence behind each varies a lot — labeled here so you can weigh it.

Diet · closest look

MCT & ketogenic-style diets

Diets built around medium-chain triglycerides (MCTs) have the best clinical-trial support of any complementary approach for canine epilepsy, reducing seizure frequency in a meaningful share of dogs with drug-resistant idiopathic epilepsy. But "ketogenic" here is doing different work than it does on a human diet plan — and it's a different animal entirely from a bag of grain-free kibble.

Why it isn't "just grain-free"

Grain-free kibble swaps out grain for another starch — usually potato, pea, or lentil — but it isn't formulated to change how the body burns fuel. A true veterinary ketogenic/MCT diet is engineered specifically to shift metabolism toward ketone production, and does it through a very different fat, not just more of it.

What's structurally differentStandard grain-free kibbleVeterinary MCT / ketogenic diet
Fat source Long-chain triglycerides (LCTs) from animal fat or plant oils — no special processing A defined share of calories from medium-chain triglycerides (mainly C8 caprylic and C10 capric fats), often from coconut- or palm-derived oil, calibrated as a percentage of metabolic energy
Carbohydrate role Grain is replaced with another starch; total carbohydrate load is often still moderate-to-high Some formulations still run 50%+ carbohydrate — the anti-seizure effect comes from the MCT fraction specifically, not from a strict low-carb ratio the way human ketogenic diets work
How it reaches the liver Digested and used like any other dietary fat MCTs bypass the usual fat-digestion route (no bile or pancreatic enzyme dependence), going straight to the liver to be converted into ketone bodies
Design intent General maintenance nutrition; "grain-free" is a marketing and ingredient choice, not a metabolic strategy Purpose-built — either a complete therapeutic diet or a measured MCT oil added to a stable base diet, dosed and monitored as part of a treatment plan
Oversight Sold over the counter with no veterinary involvement expected Best introduced with veterinary guidance — bloodwork before and during the switch, since it changes fat metabolism significantly

When it's a poor fit

Caution  A high-fat ketogenic or MCT diet is a relative or absolute poor fit for dogs with certain co-existing conditions — check with your vet before switching if your dog has any of the following.
▲

History of pancreatitis

Higher dietary fat is a recognized trigger for pancreatitis flare-ups, and dogs on potassium bromide already carry extra pancreatitis risk — stacking both is a real concern.

▲

Hyperlipidemia

Breeds prone to idiopathic high blood fats (Miniature Schnauzers, for instance) can see triglycerides climb further on a high-fat plan.

▲

Fat-intolerant GI disease

Conditions like intestinal lymphangiectasia or chronic fat-sensitive GI disease tend to worsen with a higher fat load.

▲

Liver disease

The liver does the work of turning MCTs into ketones — added metabolic burden isn't ideal on top of existing hepatic disease or phenobarbital's own liver effects.

▲

Obesity-prone dogs

These diets are calorie-dense; dogs that gain weight easily need closer portion and weight monitoring if trying one.

If a full diet change isn't a good fit: alternatives

A full therapeutic diet isn't the only way to get an MCT effect — smaller, more targeted options exist and are easier to back out of if they don't agree with your dog.

Closest substitute

MCT oil as an add-on supplement

A clinical trial found a measured MCT oil supplement — added to a dog's existing stable diet at roughly 9% of daily metabolic energy — reduced seizure frequency without requiring a full diet switch. This keeps the therapeutic ingredient while sidestepping a bigger overall fat and calorie change.

Lower fat load

Fish oil (omega-3)

Doesn't drive ketosis the way MCTs do, but is a much lower-fat way to support general neurological and cardiac health if a high-fat diet is off the table. Confirm dosing with your vet, since fish oil affects clotting.

Supports fat metabolism

L-carnitine

Helps shuttle fatty acids into cells for energy use and has been studied alongside MCT supplementation; sometimes used to support metabolism in dogs where a bigger dietary fat shift isn't appropriate.

Other complementary approaches

Cannabidiol

CBD

Small veterinary trials suggest CBD oil can reduce seizure frequency as an add-on in some dogs, though results are mixed and dosing isn't standardized. Quality and THC content vary enormously between products — a real safety issue if you're considering it, and one to raise with your vet rather than choose alone.

Bodywork

Acupuncture & chiropractic

Used by some integrative vets as an adjunct for general wellbeing and stress reduction. Evidence specific to seizure reduction in dogs is thin and largely anecdotal, though it's generally considered low-risk when performed by a credentialed veterinary practitioner.

Nutraceuticals

Omega-3 fatty acids & taurine

Proposed to support neuronal membrane stability; commonly added for general neurological and cardiac health. Not shown on their own to meaningfully reduce seizure frequency, but low-risk as a supporting supplement — confirm dosing with your vet since fish oil can interact with clotting.

06 · Emerging theories

Where the research is heading

These aren't settled standards of care yet — they're active research directions worth knowing about, especially if your dog is drug-resistant.

Gut–brain axis

The microbiome connection

Studies comparing fecal bacteria in epileptic vs. non-epileptic dogs have found distinct baseline microbiome patterns, and ketogenic diets appear to shift that microbiome alongside their anti-seizure effect. It's an open question whether the microbiome is a cause, a marker, or a lever for future probiotic-style treatments.

Precision dosing

Pharmacokinetic modeling

Newer population pharmacokinetic models aim to predict an individual dog's phenobarbital dose from weight and age up front, instead of a flat starting dose followed by trial-and-error blood draws — getting dogs to a therapeutic level faster and with fewer rechecks.

Immune-mediated component

Neuroinflammation

Researchers are investigating whether ongoing immune activity in the brain contributes to some cases of drug-resistant epilepsy, which is prompting early exploratory interest in anti-inflammatory and immune-modulating adjuncts — still experimental, not a standard treatment.

Wearables & detection

Automated seizure detection

Collar-based sensors and phone-based accelerometer apps are being trialed to detect generalized tonic-clonic seizures in real time from movement signatures, aiming to catch nighttime or unwitnessed events and time-stamp them automatically for your vet.

Not built for focal seizures: these devices work by detecting the vibration and movement pattern of convulsive activity. A focal seizure — facial twitching, a single limb, or an odd behavioral episode — often doesn't produce enough motion for the sensor to register, so a "quiet" collar reading doesn't rule one out. They're a supplement to watching your dog, not a replacement for it.

08 · Comorbidities

What tends to travel with it

Epilepsy rarely shows up alone. Some of the following come from the seizures themselves, some from the drugs used to control them.

01

Anxiety & behavior change

Increased fearfulness, noise sensitivity, or clinginess is common, and may relate to the seizures, the anticonvulsants, or both.

02

Cognitive changes

Dogs with epilepsy, especially with frequent or severe seizures, appear more prone to cognitive decline over time.

03

Orthopedic injury

Falls and disorientation during or after a seizure can cause bruising or, less often, more serious injury.

04

Liver stress

Phenobarbital is processed by the liver and can elevate liver enzymes over time, which is why periodic bloodwork is standard.

05

Pancreatitis risk

Potassium bromide has been linked to a higher risk of pancreatitis in some dogs, particularly alongside a fatty diet.

06

Weight gain

Increased appetite from phenobarbital, combined with sedation-related lower activity, makes weight creep common.

07

GI upset

Vomiting or reduced appetite shows up with several ASDs, particularly zonisamide, and usually needs dose or timing adjustment rather than stopping the drug.

08

Sedation & ataxia

Wobbliness and drowsiness are dose-related and often ease after the first few weeks as the dog adjusts.

09 · What to avoid

Contraindicated medications & supplements

Some drugs and supplements either lower the seizure threshold outright or interfere with how anticonvulsants are metabolized. Always tell every vet — including at the ER or groomer's flea/tick counter — that your dog has epilepsy.

Flag this Confirm any new medication or supplement with your prescribing vet before giving it, even something sold over the counter.
SubstanceCategoryWhy it's a concern
Tramadol Pain medication Recognized to lower the seizure threshold; generally avoided or used with extra caution in epileptic dogs.
Fluoroquinolone antibiotics Antibiotic Can lower seizure threshold, particularly at higher doses or in dogs with kidney impairment.
Certain isoxazoline flea/tick products (Bravecto, Nexgard, Simparica) Parasite prevention The FDA has flagged post-marketing reports of seizures and tremors in some dogs, including those with no prior seizure history; vets often weigh alternatives or extra caution in known epileptics.
High-dose ivermectin & related macrocyclic lactones Antiparasitic Neurotoxic at high doses, especially in dogs with the MDR1 (ABCB1) gene mutation common in herding breeds — a risk independent of epilepsy but compounded by it.
Metronidazole (high dose / prolonged use) Antibiotic/antiprotozoal Linked to neurotoxicity at higher doses, which can look like or worsen seizure activity.
Acepromazine Sedative Historically avoided in epileptic dogs on a theoretical seizure-threshold concern; many neurologists now consider low doses reasonably safe, but it's a "check with your vet first" drug.
Ginkgo biloba, evening primrose oil, borage oil Herbal supplement Contain compounds associated with lowering seizure threshold in some reports; generally avoided in epileptic dogs.
Ephedra / ma huang, and other stimulant herbal blends Herbal stimulant Stimulant herbs can provoke seizures outright and have no place in an epileptic dog's supplement routine.
Xylitol Sweetener (toxin, not a treatment) Causes a rapid blood sugar crash that can trigger seizures in any dog — worth flagging since it hides in peanut butter, gum, and "sugar-free" treats.
Grapefruit / grapefruit juice Food Affects the same liver enzyme pathway (CYP3A) used to clear several anticonvulsants, which can push blood drug levels higher than intended.

This list isn't exhaustive, and individual risk depends on the specific drug your dog is already taking — phenobarbital in particular alters the metabolism of many other medications in both directions. Always give your vet a full, current medication and supplement list.

Written as a starting point for conversations with your veterinarian or veterinary neurologist — not a replacement for either. Treatment plans should always be built around your individual dog's bloodwork, imaging, and response to treatment.
