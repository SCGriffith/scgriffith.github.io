04 · Conventional care

Daily antiseizure medications

Most dogs with idiopathic epilepsy are managed, not cured, with daily medication. Your vet chooses based on how well it works, side effects, and how easily blood levels can be checked.

MedicationRoleWhat owners noticeTiming and monitoring
Phenobarbital First-line. The most prescribed and most studied option. More thirst, appetite, and urination. Early sleepiness that usually eases. Usually given every 12 hours. Blood levels take 2 to 3 weeks to settle. Bloodwork (including liver tests) every 6 to 12 months, sooner if your dog seems unusually tired or stops eating. Missed timing matters less than with some medications, but keep it consistent.
Potassium bromide First-line. Often paired with phenobarbital for harder-to-control seizures. Sleepiness, wobbliness, and more thirst. Takes months to reach steady levels. Give with food at the same time each day. Keep your dog's salt intake and food steady: new treats or a new food can change how much bromide is absorbed. Blood level is checked about 3 months after starting, then every 6 to 12 months. Kidney disease can also change levels.
Levetiracetam (Keppra) First-line or add-on. Often chosen for fewer side effects. Usually well tolerated. Occasional mild sleepiness or upset stomach. Immediate-release tablets are usually given every 8 hours. Dogs on phenobarbital may need higher doses, so your vet may adjust them. Blood levels are not routinely needed, so dosing is based on how your dog responds.
Keppra extended-release (Keppra ER) Same drug with a slower release, so it is given less often. Similar to regular Keppra. Give at the same time every day, usually every 12 hours. Swallow the tablet whole. Do not split, crush, or chew it, because that breaks the slow-release coating. The tablets are large, so ask your vet whether they fit your dog.
Zonisamide Add-on, or an alternative first-line option if phenobarbital is not tolerated. Used off-label in dogs, meaning not specifically approved for them. Vomiting, sleepiness, or wobbliness. Rarely, liver or kidney effects. Given every 12 hours, usually with food. A blood level is often checked 1 to 2 weeks after starting or changing the dose. Liver and kidney bloodwork before starting, then periodically. Do not stop suddenly. Phenobarbital speeds up how fast zonisamide clears, so your vet may need to raise the zonisamide dose when both are used.
Imepitoin (Pexion) Approved in the European Union for idiopathic epilepsy. In the United States it is approved for noise anxiety, so use for epilepsy is off-label. Mild sleepiness, wobbliness, vomiting, or increased appetite. Given twice a day, about 12 hours apart, at a consistent time relative to meals. Not recommended as the main treatment for clusters or status epilepticus. Not suited to dogs with serious liver, kidney, or heart disease.
Pregabalin Add-on for dogs not fully controlled on a main medication. Sleepiness and wobbliness. Cleared by the kidneys, so kidney health affects dosing.
05 · Rescue medications

Stopping a seizure that has started

Rescue medications are used during a seizure, not every day. They are for clusters, status epilepticus, or seizures your dog has repeatedly had close together. Ask your vet whether one is right for your dog and how to use it.

Fastest option at home

Intranasal midazolam

Midazolam is a fast-acting medication that calms overactive brain activity. "Intranasal" means it is given into the nose, where it is absorbed quickly. It is often given at home when a seizure starts or when a cluster begins.

How fast it works: In studies, seizures usually stopped within about 30 to 100 seconds. Dogs often start to relax within about 5 minutes.

What you will see: Body becomes less stiff, jerking slows and stops, and your dog becomes very relaxed and sleepy. Some wobbliness (called ataxia) is normal. Rest after a seizure helps the brain recover.

How long it lasts: The medication works for at least 15 minutes. Sleepiness can last several hours.

How well it works: In a clinical study, intranasal midazolam stopped status epilepticus in about 76% of cases. In an owner survey, 97% of owners said it worked well.

Talk to your vet about a prescription if your dog has frequent seizures or seizures that are hard to stop. Your vet will show you exactly how to use it.

Hands-on technique

Ocular compression (gentle eye pressure)

Gentle pressure on the eyes can stimulate the vagus nerve, a large nerve that runs from the brain to the stomach. This may help calm a seizure or shorten it.

How to do it: Hold your dog's head steady. Close the eyelids and press firmly but gently with your thumbs or fingers, about as firm as checking a pulse. Press for 5 to 8 seconds, release for 5 to 8 seconds, and repeat for up to 10 minutes or until the seizure stops.

Research so far: In a study of 7 dogs, pressure stopped seizures in 3, and in 2 more dogs prevented seizures. It may also shorten seizures and ease confusion afterward.

Important: Have your vet teach you this technique before you need it. Tell your vet about any eye problems or injuries first.

More detail is on the Alternative & emerging page.

When to call your vet or go to the emergency clinic

Immediately, for any of these: A seizure lasting more than 5 minutes. Two or more seizures within 24 hours (a cluster). A seizure that does not stop after rescue medication. Breathing trouble, or a dog that does not wake normally after a seizure.

Before any daily medication change, ask your vet about a rescue plan so you are not planning in the middle of an emergency.
