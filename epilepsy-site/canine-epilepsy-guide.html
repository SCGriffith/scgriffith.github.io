A field guide for owners

The electrical storm, explained.

Epilepsy is the most common long-term (chronic) brain condition in dogs. This guide explains how seizures are classified, what treats them, what is still research, and how to keep the record your vet needs.

01 · Overview

What "epilepsy" covers

Epilepsy is a tendency to have repeated seizures with no obvious trigger. Vets sort the cause into three main groups before choosing treatment.

Most common

Idiopathic epilepsy

"Idiopathic" means no cause is found. Bloodwork and brain scans come back normal, and a genetic or inherited basis is suspected. Most dogs diagnosed with epilepsy fall into this group. Canine epilepsy can affect up to 5% of dogs, according to the manufacturer of a veterinary seizure medication.

Has a visible cause

Structural epilepsy

Seizures come from a problem you can see on a scan, such as a tumor, past head injury, stroke, a brain malformation, or infection. This is more likely when seizures start outside the usual age range or when the dog has abnormal findings on exam.

Not epilepsy itself

Reactive seizures

The brain is normal but is reacting to a body problem, such as low blood sugar, liver or kidney disease, a salt imbalance, or a toxin. Treating the underlying problem is the fix, not a daily seizure medication.

How vets sort it out

The diagnostic process

Bloodwork and sometimes bile acid tests rule out reactive causes. MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) and spinal fluid tests look for structural disease. Idiopathic epilepsy is the diagnosis when a dog fits the usual pattern and everything else comes back normal.

02 · The first-seizure question

One seizure: watch, or start treatment?

A single brief seizure does not automatically mean lifelong daily medication. Deciding whether to start, and later whether to reduce, follows its own logic.

Antiseizure drug (also called an anticonvulsant): a daily medication that prevents seizures. This is different from a rescue medication, which is used to stop a seizure that has already started.

Common approach

"Watch and wait" after one event

Consensus guidance for dogs is extrapolated from strong human evidence. It finds no proven long-term benefit to starting daily medication after a single unprovoked seizure. Dogs who wait tend to reach the same long-term seizure control as dogs treated right away.

Waiting is not doing nothing. The goal is to find out whether the seizure was a one-time reaction (to stress, illness, a toxin, or low blood sugar) or the start of ongoing epilepsy. Keep a careful log (see the Tracking Seizures page) so your vet can see whether it happens again.

When vets start sooner

Starting medication right away

Your vet may start medication immediately if the first event was a cluster (two or more seizures in 24 hours), if it was status epilepticus (described below), if a scan shows brain disease, or if seizures are closely spaced from the start.

About reducing medication

Tapering is not a diagnostic test

After a long seizure-free period, your vet may consider slowly lowering medication to see whether it is still needed. This is a separate decision from the "one seizure: start or wait" question. Any reduction should be gradual, planned, and supervised by your vet.

Why this matters

Stopping suddenly is dangerous

Stopping or rapidly lowering medications like phenobarbital or potassium bromide can trigger withdrawal seizures or status epilepticus. Any change should take weeks to months and be monitored closely.

Plan ahead Before any medication change, ask your vet about a rescue medication plan, such as intranasal midazolam (described on the Medications & Rescue page), in case a seizure happens while doses are being adjusted.
03 · Seizure types

Generalized vs. focal: where the seizure starts

Vets classify seizures by where in the brain they start. That affects what the seizure looks like and sometimes which medication works best.

Most recognizable

Generalized seizures

Both sides of the brain are involved from the start. This is the seizure most owners picture.

  • The dog collapses onto its side and loses consciousness
  • Stiffening (tonic phase), then paddling or jerking legs (clonic phase)
  • Drooling, chomping, and sometimes loss of bladder or bowel control
  • Usually lasts under 2 minutes. Longer than 5 minutes is an emergency

Easy to miss

Focal seizures

Only one area of the brain fires. The signs are small and easy to mistake for behavior, which is why they are often under-reported.

  • Facial twitching, one leg jerking, or repeated eye blinking
  • Sudden unusual behavior: snapping at the air, licking the air, sudden fear or aggression
  • The dog often stays awake and aware
  • It may stay focal, or spread to involve the whole body (see below)

Why a focal seizure can still be "focal" after it spreads

A focal seizure can spread to other parts of the brain, including both sides. When it becomes a full-body seizure, this is called a "secondary generalized" seizure or a focal-to-bilateral tonic-clonic seizure. It is still classified as focal, because the seizure is named for where it started, not for how far it spread. Think of a fire that starts in one room and spreads through the house: it is still a fire that started in that room.

Tracking focal seizures

Note whether each focal seizure stays in one spot or spreads to the whole body, and how long before it spreads. Your vet can use this pattern to guide treatment. Video is especially useful for focal seizures because they are hard to describe accurately afterward. The Tracking Seizures page explains what to capture.

Clusters and status epilepticus

These two terms describe emergency patterns. Both can happen with focal or generalized seizures. The sources I reviewed did not break these down by where the seizure starts.

Emergency

Clusters

Two or more seizures within 24 hours, with the dog returning to normal between them. Clusters can be a warning sign that a longer emergency may follow. Call your vet right away if this happens.

Emergency

Status epilepticus

One seizure lasting more than 5 minutes, or two or more seizures without the dog returning to normal in between. This is a life-threatening emergency. Treatment is needed right away, and emergency care is required.

Five minutes is the emergency line used by the American College of Veterinary Internal Medicine's consensus statement on seizure emergencies.

Body temperature during a seizure

Muscle activity during a generalized seizure produces heat, much like hard exercise does. A brief seizure rarely causes dangerous overheating, but long seizures, clusters, or status epilepticus can raise temperature quickly. Overheating then becomes its own emergency.

Normal is your dog's usual resting range. Elevated is common after prolonged muscle activity. It is worth watching but is not an emergency on its own. Severe is heatstroke territory. The American Animal Hospital Association describes heatstroke as body temperature rising to dangerous levels, usually above 104°F, which can damage organs. Critical (above about 106°F or 41°C) carries a sharp risk of organ damage and death. This needs emergency vet care right away.

If you start cooling your dog at home on the way to the vet, stop once the temperature drops to about 103°F. Cooling further can cause the temperature to drop too low. Let the vet team take it from there.
