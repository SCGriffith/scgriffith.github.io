08 · Comorbidities

What tends to travel with it

Epilepsy rarely shows up alone. Some of these come from the seizures themselves, and some come from the medications used to control them.

01

Anxiety and behavior change

Fearfulness, sensitivity to noise, or clinginess is common. It may come from the seizures, the medication, or both.

02

Thinking and memory changes

Dogs with frequent or severe seizures appear more likely to show memory and thinking decline over time.

03

Injury from falls

Falls and confusion during or after a seizure can cause bruising or, less often, more serious injury.

04

Liver strain

Phenobarbital is processed by the liver and can raise liver values over time. That is why regular bloodwork is standard.

05

Pancreatitis risk

Potassium bromide has been linked to a higher risk of pancreatitis (painful inflammation of the pancreas) in some dogs, especially with a high-fat diet.

06

Weight gain

Phenobarbital can increase appetite, and sleepiness means less activity. Weight can creep up.

07

Stomach upset

Vomiting or reduced appetite can occur with several medications, especially zonisamide. It usually calls for a dose or timing change rather than stopping the drug.

08

Sleepiness and wobbliness

These are dose-related and often ease over the first few weeks as the dog adjusts.

09 · What to avoid

Medications and products with real risks

Some drugs and supplements lower the seizure threshold (make seizures more likely) or interfere with how seizure medications are processed. Tell every vet, including emergency clinics, that your dog has epilepsy.

Check first Confirm any new medication or supplement with your prescribing vet before giving it, even if it is sold without a prescription.
SubstanceTypeWhy it is a concern
TramadolPain medicationKnown to lower the seizure threshold. Generally avoided or used with extra caution in epileptic dogs.
Fluoroquinolone antibioticsAntibioticCan lower the seizure threshold, especially at higher doses or in dogs with kidney impairment.
Some isoxazoline flea and tick products (Bravecto, NexGard, Simparica)Parasite preventionThe U.S. Food and Drug Administration has flagged reports of seizures and tremors in some dogs, including dogs with no earlier seizure history. Vets often weigh alternatives or use extra caution in dogs with epilepsy.
High-dose ivermectin and related drugsAntiparasiticCan be toxic to the nervous system at high doses, especially in dogs with the MDR1 gene variant common in herding breeds.
Metronidazole (high dose or long-term use)AntibioticLinked to nervous system toxicity at higher doses, which can look like or worsen seizures.
AcepromazineSedativeHistorically avoided in epileptic dogs. Many neurologists now consider low doses reasonably safe, but it should be discussed with your vet first.
Ginkgo biloba, evening primrose oil, borage oilHerbal supplementContain compounds linked to lowering the seizure threshold in some reports. Generally avoided in epileptic dogs.
Ephedra and other stimulant herbal blendsHerbal stimulantCan trigger seizures directly and have no place in an epileptic dog's supplement routine.
XylitolSweetener (toxic)Causes a rapid drop in blood sugar that can trigger seizures in any dog. It hides in peanut butter, gum, and "sugar-free" treats.
Grapefruit and grapefruit juiceFoodAffects the liver pathway used to clear several seizure medications, which can raise drug levels higher than intended.

This list is not complete. Risk depends on which medications your dog takes. Phenobarbital in particular changes how the body processes many other drugs. Always give your vet a full, current list of medications and supplements.
