05 · Alternative & adjunct approaches

Complementary options, honestly labeled

None of these replace a daily antiseizure medication in a dog that needs one. They are used alongside standard treatment. The evidence behind each one varies a lot, so each is labeled.

Diet · best studied

Medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) diets and oils

Medium-chain triglycerides are a type of fat that the body processes differently from most dietary fats. They can be added to a diet as a food product or as a measured oil. Several controlled studies in dogs with epilepsy have tested them, and they have the strongest evidence of any complementary option here. The effect is modest: most dogs in these trials saw a partial reduction in seizures, and a minority became seizure-free. MCT is not a cure and does not replace medication.

What the studies found

StudyWhat was testedResults
Berk et al., 2015 (Br J Nutr) Ketogenic medium-chain triglyceride diet vs. a placebo diet, for 3 months each, in dogs already on medication Seizures were lower on the medium-chain diet. Three dogs became seizure-free, and seven more had at least a 50% reduction.
Berk et al., 2020 (J Vet Intern Med) Medium-chain oil added to the diet at about 9% of daily energy, compared with a control oil, for 3 months each. 28 dogs. Two dogs were seizure-free, three had at least a 50% reduction, and twelve had a smaller reduction. Eleven showed no change.
Volk et al., 2020 (Vet Record) A commercial diet containing 6.5% medium-chain oil in 21 dogs, without cluster seizures, across 10 European clinics. Not blinded. Average seizure frequency dropped by about 32%, and seizure days dropped by about 42%.
Purina NeuroCare trial (Volk, supported by Nestlé Purina) A commercial diet formulated with medium-chain triglycerides, given alongside existing medication Significant reductions in seizure frequency were reported. Purina funded this study, so treat the result with caution.

Why it is not "just grain-free"

Grain-free food swaps grain for another starch, usually potato, pea, or lentil. It does not change how the body fuels itself. A veterinary medium-chain diet is designed to shift the body toward making ketones (an alternative fuel), using a different kind of fat.

Monitoring while on a medium-chain diet or oil

Bloodwork

Baseline bloodwork before starting, then periodic liver and fat (lipid) checks. Dogs with other conditions or long-term use need closer checks.

Seizure log

Keep a daily log for several months, both before and after starting. Without a baseline, you cannot tell whether the change helped.

Body weight

Weigh your dog monthly or track body condition. These products are calorie-dense, so weight can rise without anyone noticing.

Stool and appetite

Watch for loose stools, vomiting, or changes in appetite, especially in the first weeks. Ask your vet how to adjust the amount if these occur.

Starting slowly

Clinicians commonly recommend starting with a small amount and increasing over one to two weeks while watching stool and appetite. This comes from clinical guidance in secondary sources, not from a controlled trial, so your vet should set the exact plan.

Sourcing

Use veterinary-formulated products or measured oils recommended by your vet, not general supplements. Quality and the exact fat content vary between brands. Medium-chain oil is not the same as coconut oil, and a generic supplement may not contain a measured dose. Ask your vet which product and amount to use.

Not a fit for every dog

Ask first A high-fat diet is not appropriate for every dog. Check with your vet before starting if your dog has any of the following.
01

History of pancreatitis

Higher fat can trigger flare-ups. Potassium bromide already carries some pancreatitis risk, so the two together need careful thought.

02

High blood fats

Some breeds, such as Miniature Schnauzers, are prone to high blood triglycerides, which can rise further on a high-fat diet.

03

Sensitive digestion

Dogs with conditions that make fat hard to digest are more likely to have problems on a higher-fat diet.

04

Liver disease

The liver does the work of converting these fats into ketones. This adds load to a liver that is already under strain.

05

Weight gain risk

These products are calorie-dense. Dogs that gain weight easily need closer portion and weight checks.

Lower-commitment options

Closest option

Measured oil added to a stable diet

The 2020 Berk study used a measured oil at about 9% of daily energy added to the usual diet. This keeps the diet mostly the same while still adding the active ingredient.

Lower fat

Fish oil (omega-3)

Does not produce ketones like medium-chain fats do. It supports general nerve and heart health. Fish oil can affect blood clotting, so ask your vet about the dose.

Metabolic support

L-carnitine

Helps move fat into cells for energy. It is sometimes used alongside medium-chain products, but there is little direct evidence for seizures in dogs.

05a · Gut-brain connection

The gut and the brain

The bacteria in your dog's gut (the microbiome) and the brain communicate in both directions. The signals travel through nerves, the bloodstream, and the immune system.

Research

What has been studied

Studies comparing stool bacteria in epileptic and healthy dogs found different patterns. Dogs with idiopathic epilepsy had fewer bacteria that make GABA, a calming chemical in the brain, and fewer that make short-chain fatty acids, which support brain health. Medium-chain diets also appear to shift the microbiome alongside their effect on seizures.

Still open

Cause, marker, or treatment target?

It is not yet known whether an unbalanced gut (called dysbiosis) causes epilepsy, reflects it, or could be treated to improve it. Probiotics and diet changes aimed at the gut are being studied but are not standard care.

Ask your vet before using probiotics or gut supplements, so they can be weighed against your dog's other medications.

05b · Eye pressure technique

Ocular compression: stimulating the vagus nerve

The vagus nerve runs from the brain to the stomach and helps regulate many body functions. Gentle pressure on the eyes is thought to stimulate it, and this may help calm abnormal brain activity.

Hands-on technique

How it is thought to work

Stimulating the vagus nerve is thought to trigger release of GABA and glycine, two natural brain chemicals that act as brakes on the abnormal electrical activity behind seizures.

Step-by-step

Performing it

1. Hold your dog's head steady. 2. Close the eyelids and press firmly but gently with your thumbs or fingers, about as firm as checking a pulse. 3. Press for 5 to 8 seconds, then release for 5 to 8 seconds. 4. Repeat for up to 10 minutes or until the seizure activity decreases.

Pressure: firm enough to stimulate, never hard enough to hurt the eyes. If you are unsure, have your vet demonstrate.

Research

What the study found

In a study of 7 dogs with seizures, pressure stopped seizures in 3 dogs, decreased heart rate and relaxed muscles in 1, and prevented seizures from starting in 2 others. It was reported to shorten seizures and reduce confusion and temporary blindness afterward.

Important

Safety

Have your vet teach you this before you need it in an emergency. Do not use it if your dog has eye problems or eye injuries without your vet's advice.

05c · Gold bead therapy

Gold bead implants

Experimental · discuss with your vet

Gold bead therapy is an acupuncture-based approach. It is not established treatment for seizures. It is described here because owners ask about it.

What it is

Tiny gold beads or short lengths of gold wire are placed under the skin at acupuncture points and left in place. Practitioners describe the result as permanent acupuncture: a needle is removed after a session, but a gold implant keeps stimulating the same point.

Why gold

Gold is chemically stable and tolerated well by body tissue, so it can stay in place for a long time. Veterinary sources describe a slow release of gold ions from the implant surface, which nearby immune cells take up. The proposed explanation is an ongoing local effect at the acupuncture point. These mechanisms were described for joint pain and inflammation, not specifically for seizures.

What the evidence shows

In 1987, a University of Pennsylvania team treated five dogs that had not responded to high-dose medication. Two dogs had fewer seizures at first, but returned to their earlier pattern about five months later. In a 2009 study of 15 dogs, 9 had at least a 50% reduction in seizures over 15 weeks. The same study found no significant change on EEG (a brain wave test). The studies are small and have no comparison group, so this evidence is weak.

Who pioneered it

Gold implants at acupuncture points in dogs were described in the early 1990s by Dr. T. E. Durkes in the United States, and gold bead implants were later used in dogs in several European countries. Veterinary sources describe the method as widely used for other conditions, including hip dysplasia. Its use in epilepsy is much less studied.

What is not known

Whether implants affect future MRI scans. Some owners report being told they cannot have MRIs after gold implants, but I could not confirm this. Ask your vet before implanting. Scar tissue forming around the beads has been suggested as a reason results may fade, but this is a theory, not a finding.

Before you decide

Talk to your vet, and ideally a veterinary neurologist, before considering gold bead therapy. Do not stop or change seizure medication because of it. Ask about the procedure, aftercare, cost, and what results would look like for your dog.

05d · Other approaches

Other complementary approaches

Cannabidiol

Cannabidiol (CBD oil)

Small veterinary trials suggest it may reduce seizures in some dogs when added to other treatment. Results are mixed, and dosing is not standardized. Products vary widely in content, and some contain the compound that causes a high. Discuss it with your vet before choosing a product.

Bodywork

Acupuncture and chiropractic care

Some integrative vets use these for general wellbeing and stress. Evidence for reducing seizures is limited and mostly anecdotal. They are generally low-risk when a qualified veterinary practitioner provides them.

Supplements

Omega-3 fatty acids and taurine

Thought to support the stability of nerve cell membranes and general brain and heart health. They have not been shown to reduce seizures on their own, but are low-risk supporting supplements. Ask your vet about dosing, since fish oil can affect clotting.

06 · Emerging theories

Where the research is heading

These are not standard care yet. They are research directions worth knowing about, especially if your dog's seizures are hard to control.

Gut-brain connection

The microbiome

Gut bacteria patterns differ in dogs with epilepsy, and medium-chain diets appear to shift them. Whether this is cause, marker, or a future treatment target is still open.

Dosing science

Predicting the right dose

New models aim to estimate a dog's phenobarbital dose from weight and age at the start, instead of trial and error with repeated blood tests.

Immune component

Inflammation in the brain

Researchers are studying whether ongoing immune activity in the brain plays a role in hard-to-control epilepsy. Anti-inflammatory approaches are experimental.

Detection technology

Automatic seizure detection

Collar sensors and smartphone apps are being tested to detect full-body seizures from movement and log them automatically.

Limitation: These detect convulsions. A focal seizure with only facial twitching or one leg movement may not register, so a quiet reading does not rule one out.
